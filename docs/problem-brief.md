# The problem SQRlane solves

Working brief. Every number here is either sourced or marked as unproven.
Nothing is invented. If a figure has no named source, it does not go in the deck.

---

## In one line

**A forwarder's booking system does not know what is really happening on the
booking. The people do. They keep it in their heads, their inboxes and their
chat apps.**

---

## What the day actually looks like

A booking is agreed over email. The rate is amended in a chat. The carrier
sends an exception notice. The customer asks for an update somewhere else
again.

None of that reaches the booking on its own. A person carries it across, one
message at a time.

That carrying costs five separate things:

**1. Finding it.** Before anyone can act, they have to find the message. Which
app, whose thread, which version.

**2. Retyping it.** Once found, it gets typed into the system by hand.

**3. Deciding what is true.** The same fact sits in three places and the three
do not agree. Someone picks one. Often they are guessing.

**4. Switching.** Four apps, four streams of notifications. The desk never gets
a clear hour.

**5. One person holding it all.** The full picture is in one head. That person
takes leave and the shipment stalls.

### The one that costs money, not just time

Number 3.

If a surcharge was agreed in a chat and never made it onto the invoice, the
forwarder pays it. That is a real loss, in euros, caused only by the
information sitting in the wrong place.

Hours are easy to shrug off. A missed surcharge is not.

## How big it is

Evidence is graded. Grade A goes on a slide. Grade B is colour, with the
company named. Grade C is rejected, and the reason is written down so nobody
puts it back.

### Grade A — safe to present

|---|---|---|
| **60% of the working day** | Goes to coordination: chasing status, searching for information, switching apps. Only 40% goes to the skilled work. | Asana, *Anatomy of Work Index* 2022 |
| **~10 apps, ~25 switches a day** | And about 4 hours a week just re-orienting after each switch. Roughly 9% of the working year. | Asana, same |

### Grade C — rejected, do not reintroduce

These numbers are everywhere and they look great:

- $4.50 to process a document by hand
- $53.50 to fix one invoice error
- a 1–4% data entry error rate
- "a 2% error margin across 500 containers costs €20,000 in margin"

**Every one traces back to a vendor blog citing another vendor blog.** None has
a primary source. They are plausible, widely repeated and unverifiable, which
is the worst combination. An investor with a logistics background will push on
one of them, and it will fall apart.

## Who actually pays

The forwarder. All of it.

This matters more than it looks. An earlier draft of the deck used BASF's
€250m and McKinsey's "45% of a year's profit". Both of those are the **cargo
owner's** loss, not the forwarder's. Using a shipper's pain to argue a
forwarder's pain is a joint that breaks under one good question.

The five costs above are different. Every one of them lands on the forwarder's
own accounts, as salary and as margin. Nothing is borrowed.

---

## Why nobody has fixed it

**Most forwarders have nothing to integrate with.** Fewer than 40% run a
forwarding management system. You cannot connect to a system that is not
there.

**The channels are conversations, not systems.** Software vendors connect
systems to systems. Nobody connected the chat, because chat is not a business
system. It is where the people already were, and the software never followed.

**The fix has always been to hire someone.** And it works. That is the problem.
It means cost grows with volume, so winning a bigger customer makes the margin
worse instead of better.

That last line is the strongest version of the whole problem. It is not an
efficiency story. It is a ceiling on the business.

## What we do not claim

**We do not name WhatsApp.** It is probably used. It matches how freight
actually works. But every source that turns up is vendor marketing about
*customer-facing* WhatsApp — tracking alerts and chatbots from DHL, Maersk and
FedEx. There is no credible evidence of it as an internal coordination channel
in forwarding. Naming it on a slide invites "so do you read WhatsApp?", and the
honest answer is a roadmap item. Say "email, chat and messaging apps" and let
the room fill in the rest.

**The 60% figure needs one sentence of care.** For a forwarder, coordination
*is* the product. Someone will say "yes, that is literally the job." The
distinction that saves it:

> Coordination that needs judgement — negotiating a rate, deciding a reroute —
> is the value the forwarder sells. Coordination that only moves information
> from one place to another — finding where the rate was agreed, retyping it —
> is not. We only attack the second.

Put that on the slide. Left unsaid, the figure argues against us.

**We sell to the digitised minority, on purpose.** Our pitch is that we sit on
top of the TMS. For most forwarders there is no TMS to sit on. That is not a
hole in the argument as long as we say it: the beachhead is forwarders who
already run a modern system. It is also why 213 named target companies out of
19,000 European firms is a sensible number rather than a suspiciously small
one.

## What we still need

Three numbers, and three customer conversations would get all of them. Each one
is worth more than any figure above, because it is ours and it is specific.

1. **Hours per booking** lost to finding and retyping.
2. **How many channels** one shipment touches, start to finish.
3. **One real miss.** A surcharge agreed in a chat that never reached the
   invoice, with the euro value attached.

Number 3 is the one to chase. A single true story with a number on it beats a
consultancy chart in every room.
